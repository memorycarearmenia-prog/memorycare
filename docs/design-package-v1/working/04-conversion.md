# 04 — Conversion & Trust
**Owner:** Conversion and Trust Strategist
**Scope:** everything between a stranger landing on the page and a paid, calm client who has received a first report.
**Language:** English (source of truth for HY / RU localisation).

---

## 0. The one sentence this document is built on

> A person who has never met us, in a different time zone, on a phone, at
> one in the morning, is deciding whether to send us the equivalent of
> USD 400 to look after their mother's grave — and nobody they know has
> ever heard of us.

Everything below is an answer to that sentence. We have **no reviews, no
history, no customers**. We therefore cannot borrow trust from other people.
We must manufacture it out of four raw materials, and only these four:

1. **Verifiable process** — GPS, timestamps, video, a named method, named equipment.
2. **Named guarantees** — specific, numeric, with a stated remedy.
3. **Real, named humans** — two founders, real phone numbers, real email, a real legal entity.
4. **Transparent, self-serve pricing** — the price is knowable before any human contact.

Also non-negotiable, from the competitive brief: **hush.am exists, since ~2015,
with photo reports, GPS and ~72 reviews.** We never claim to be the only ones,
the first ones, or the only ones with photo reports. Our claim is the *complete
chain* — photo + video + GPS + portal + family circle — and the rigour around it.
Written correctly, that is a stronger and safer claim than a superlative.

## A2. The trust ladder — Inspection → Express → Subscription

The ladder is our answer to having no reviews: we let the client buy the proof
in increments instead of buying it on faith. The site's job is to make each rung
look like the obvious next step, and to make it impossible to read Inspection as
"the cheap version".

**Rung 1 — Inspection, 20,000 ֏.** Framed as *"Find out what your plot needs."*
Not as care. Its deliverable is knowledge: located plot, recorded GPS point,
written condition record, photo + video, and a priced list of recommended work.
The pitch: *the smallest amount of money that converts "I don't know what's
happening there" into "I know exactly what's happening there."* It also
quietly does our operational work — it fixes the plot's coordinates.

**Rung 2 — Express, 65,000 ֏.** Framed as *"Have it done once, properly."*
One full visit, deep clean, report, portal access. The bridge from knowledge to
result.

**Rung 3 — the annual subscriptions.** Framed as *"Stop deciding every time."*
Optimal, 4 visits, one per season, 160,000 ֏/yr — the leading choice.
Maximum, 6 visits, 200,000 ֏/yr.

**How the site makes each step feel inevitable:**

1. **The credit is stated on the lower rung, not the higher one.** The Inspection
   card itself says: *"Credited toward an annual subscription if you subscribe
   within 60 days."* A client reads the risk-removal at the moment of hesitation,
   not after they have already scrolled past. Same line on Express with its own
   figure.
2. **Credit rules stated exactly, once, in plain words**, immediately under the
   tier row: *"When you sign an annual subscription we credit one one-off service
   you have already paid for — Inspection or Express, whichever is larger, not
   both — if it was paid within the previous 60 days. One-off services do not
   credit into each other, and Express is always 65,000 ֏."* Ambiguity here
   generates support load and refund disputes; precision here reads as honesty.
3. **The report is the rung-to-rung bridge.** Every one-off delivers a real
   report in the portal. The upgrade prompt therefore never has to be a sales
   message — it is a *state* the client is already inside. Placement rule: the
   upgrade offer appears in the portal on the visit-list screen and in the
   follow-up email, **never inside the report view**, and never on a guest view.
4. **A named 60-day countdown, shown as a fact not a threat.** In the portal
   after a one-off: *"Your 20,000 ֏ is credited toward an annual subscription
   until 14 November."* No red timer, no scarcity theatre — that register would
   destroy the brand.
5. **The ladder is drawn on the page.** A three-step horizontal strip on the
   pricing page — Know → Do once → Keep it cared for — with the price on each
   step. People convert to a *path* more readily than to a *product*.

## A3. CTA strategy

**Primary CTA, site-wide, one wording only:** **Request a free consultation.**
Same words everywhere. Varying the label ("Get started", "Contact us", "Book a
call") splits the recognition and measurably costs requests.

**Secondary CTA, by page:**
- Home: *See a sample report* (a proof action — for this business, viewing the
  report is a genuine micro-conversion and predicts the request).
- Pricing: *Calculate my price* (anchors to the calculator), and *Pay online*
  as a tertiary, clearly marked "bank transfer for now".
- Sample report / Family Circle: *Request a free consultation*.

**Count and placement.**
- **Home:** 4 primary placements + 1 sticky. Hero (above the fold at 375px),
  after How it works, after Guarantees, in the closing block. Plus a sticky
  bottom bar on mobile from the moment the hero scrolls out. No more. A page
  with a CTA every screen reads as pressure and this brand cannot afford
  pressure.
- **Pricing:** one CTA on each tier card (*Request a consultation*), one under
  the calculator (which becomes contextual — *Request a consultation for
  24 m², 3 monuments*, pre-filling the form), one in the closing block.
- **Every other page:** one primary at the end, plus the sticky mobile bar.
- **Header:** a phone number, not a button. On mobile, a tap-to-call icon.
  For the 55-year-old in Glendale, the phone *is* the CTA.

**Never:** a CTA inside the report view, on a guest report, or next to a
photograph of a grave. Absolute.

**What happens after submit** — designed, not left to a default:
1. The form does not navigate away. It is replaced in place by a confirmation
   card (no page reload — a reload on a slow mobile connection reads as failure).
2. The confirmation card states, in this order: (a) *We have your request.*
   (b) **What happens next and when** — "Davit or Hayk will call you within one
   working day. Our hours are 10:00–19:00 Yerevan time — that is 01:00–10:00 in
   Los Angeles." Compute and show the visitor's local equivalent from their
   browser timezone. (c) *If you would rather not wait:* WhatsApp link and the
   two direct mobile numbers. (d) *What the call is:* 10–15 minutes, we locate
   the plot, tell you what it needs, quote it, no obligation.
3. An immediate email confirmation from info@memorycare.am repeating the same
   four items — the diaspora buyer's proof that a real system received the
   request.
4. Failure state designed too: if submission fails, the card shows the two
   mobile numbers and the WhatsApp link as a fallback. A lost request is a lost
   client; never let an error dead-end.

## A4. The consultation form

**Four fields. Two required.** Every additional field costs requests, and this
form is the entire commercial funnel.

| Field | Required | Type | Why it earns its place |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Name** | Yes | text | We are going to phone a person about their mother. We must say a name. Label "Your name", single field — do not split first/last; Armenian naming and diaspora forms both suffer from that split. |
| **Phone or email** | Yes | single field, either accepted | Forcing a phone number loses the privacy-cautious diaspora client; forcing email loses the older local client who lives on WhatsApp. One field, validated as either. Must accept **international formats** — +1, +33, +7, +374 — with a country-code selector defaulted from IP/locale, never a hard `+374` mask. A US number rejected by the form is a client lost permanently and silently. |
| **Cemetery or city** | Yes | free text with autocomplete over known Yerevan cemeteries, free entry allowed | The one field that changes the *quality* of the callback: it tells us whether we can serve them at all and lets the caller open with something specific. Autocomplete, never a closed dropdown — the client may know the cemetery by a colloquial name. |
| **Anything you'd like us to know** | No | textarea, collapsed behind a link | Optional and visually secondary. It costs nothing when collapsed and it captures the emotionally loaded context ("my father is buried there, my mother next to him") that makes the first call human. Never required. |

**Explicitly rejected fields and why:** plot size and monument count (that is the
calculator's job, and asking is a quiz the client will fail), preferred contact
time (guessed wrong more often than right; ask on the call), how did you hear
about us (attribution belongs in analytics, not in the client's way), the name of
the deceased (asking a stranger to type that into a web form before any human
contact is a register violation and will cost requests), and any consent
checkbox beyond a single-line privacy statement with a link.

**Mechanics:** no CAPTCHA visible to the user (honeypot + timing instead — a
CAPTCHA at 1 a.m. on a phone is a real loss channel); inline validation on blur,
never on keystroke; the submit button says **Request a free consultation**, never
"Submit"; the button shows a busy state and cannot be double-fired; and one line
of reassurance directly under the button: *"No payment now. No account needed."*

## A5. The guarantees block

**Where it sits — in three places, deliberately:**
1. **Home**, immediately after How it works and before the closing CTA. It is
   the last thing the buyer reads before deciding, and it is our substitute for
   testimonials, so it takes the place testimonials would have occupied.
2. **Pricing**, directly beneath the tier row and above the calculator — the
   moment the number is on screen is the moment the risk question fires.
3. **Portal**, as a permanent, quiet link in the visit-list screen — the
   guarantee must be reachable when it is actually needed, not only when we are
   selling.

**How it is phrased.** Legal boilerplate reads as self-protection; guarantees
read as substance when they have four properties: a **name**, a **number**, a
**stated remedy**, and an **honest limit**. Three items, no more.

> ### MemoryCare Guarantees
>
> **Not satisfied? We come back within 7 days.**
> If a report shows work you consider unfinished, tell us and we return and redo
> it. No charge, no argument. Say so within 7 days of receiving the report.
>
> **If we damage a monument, we put it right.**
> We photograph and record the condition of every monument before we touch it,
> and we choose pressure and chemistry for the material. If we damage something,
> we restore or replace it at our own cost.
>
> **Cancel and we return the unused part of the year.**
> An annual subscription can be cancelled at any time. We refund the visits you
> have not received, pro-rata. You can do it yourself in the portal — you do not
> have to phone us.

Then, in smaller Anthracite text, the honest limit — this is what converts the
block from marketing into substance:

> *What these do not cover: damage that was already there and recorded in the
> first report; work not included in your plan; weather or cemetery access
> conditions, where we reschedule and tell you rather than skip. Full terms:
> Refund policy, Terms of service, Service limitations.*

**Phrasing rules:** second person ("we", "you"), never "the Company" or "the
Client". Present tense. Every guarantee starts with the client's situation, not
our obligation. Numbers as digits (7 days, pro-rata) — digits read as commitments,
words read as sentiment.

## A6. Pricing page decision architecture

**Target: a person chooses in under 60 seconds, on a 375px screen.**

The failure mode of a five-product page is that the client compares five things
and chooses none. The fix is that they never compare five — they answer **two
questions in sequence**, each with a small number of options.

**Question 1 (the fork, at the top of the page): "Do you already know what your plot needs?"**

Two doors, side by side on desktop, stacked on mobile:
- *No, I haven't seen it in years* → **Inspection, 20,000 ֏, one-off**
- *Yes — I want it cared for* → the three annual plans below

This is the mechanism that keeps Inspection from reading as a cheap tier: **it is
never placed in the same row as the subscriptions and is never priced against
them.** It sits above them, in its own card, on a different surface (Ivory card
on the Nude ground versus the plans block on its own band), labelled
**ONE-OFF · NOT A SUBSCRIPTION** in small caps, with a distinct card shape — and
crucially with a *different verb*: subscription cards say "care", the Inspection
card says "find out". Different job, not less of the same job. Its price is set
in the same size as the others, not smaller — shrinking it is what makes a thing
read as cheap.

Express sits with Inspection in the one-off band, as its sibling: *find out* /
*do it once*. So the top band has two one-offs, the lower band three
subscriptions — and no card is ever compared across a band boundary.

**Question 2 (inside the subscriptions band): "How often?"**

Only two real options, and they differ on exactly one axis: **4 visits a year,
one per season (Optimal, 160,000 ֏)** or **6 visits a year (Maximum, 200,000 ֏)**.
One variable, two values — that is a sub-10-second decision. Optimal carries a
quiet "Most chosen" marker (never "bestseller"), rendered as an Olive band with
Anthracite text or a Deep Olive outline — never as a shouting badge.

**Special** is deliberately *not* a fourth card in the row. It is a single line
below the calculator: *"Larger plot, several family plots, or a case that doesn't
fit? We price it after an Inspection."* Presenting Special as a peer card adds a
fifth option to the comparison and slows every visitor down to serve a minority.
It is reached through the calculator ceiling and through the Inspection route,
exactly as the product rules require.

**Comparison mechanics.**
- Every card carries the same five rows in the same order: visits per year, what
  a visit includes, report, portal, price. Same order, same wording, so the eye
  can diff vertically without reading.
- "Every visit is a full visit" is stated **once, above the cards**, as a plain
  statement of policy — not repeated per card. Never the words "light",
  "preventive" or "monthly".
- Price format: `160,000 ֏ / year` with `AMD 160,000` in the line beneath (the
  bank requires the currency in words; the diaspora reader needs it too). An
  approximate USD/EUR line, marked *approximate — you are charged in AMD*.
- Plot coverage stated on the cards: *up to 16 m², up to 2 monuments* — then the
  calculator immediately below handles everything else. Stating the limit on the
  card and the surcharge in the calculator is what prevents the "there will be a
  hidden charge" fear.

**The calculator** sits directly beneath the plans, with the honest-pricing
sentence as its heading: **"One price list. The same price for a client in
Yerevan and a client in Los Angeles."** Two sliders, live total, the surcharge
arithmetic shown as it is applied (`160,000 + 8 m² × 10,000 + 1 monument ×
30,000 = 270,000 ֏ / year`) — showing the arithmetic, not just the total, is what
makes it read as a price list rather than a quote generated for *you*. Past
100 m² or 10 monuments it stops and offers the consultation route via Inspection.
Its output feeds the CTA: the consultation form arrives pre-filled with the
configuration.

**Page order, top to bottom:** honest-price statement → the fork → one-off band
(Inspection, Express) → subscriptions band (Optimal marked, Maximum) → credit
rules → calculator → **Guarantees** → payment reality (bank transfer now, cards
soon, no date promised) → closing CTA → footer with contacts.

## A7. The moment after payment, before the first visit

**This is the highest-risk moment in the entire product**, and it is invisible in
most specifications because it contains no screen anybody wants to design. The
client has just wired money abroad to a company with no reviews, and now
*nothing happens for days*. Every doubt they suppressed to make the payment
returns with nothing to argue against. This is where refund requests, angry
WhatsApp messages and one-star word of mouth are born — before we have done
anything wrong.

**Rule: the gap between payment and first visit must never be empty.** We fill it
with five scheduled, concrete signals.

1. **Within minutes — payment confirmation.** Not a receipt. A confirmation of
   *what happens next*, with a named human and a date: "Received, 160,000 ֏.
   Davit will call you within one working day to confirm the plot and schedule
   your first visit." Plus the invoice as a PDF for the bank-transfer path.
2. **Within one working day — a human contact.** A call or WhatsApp message from
   a named founder. At this stage, with this volume, this is affordable and it is
   the single highest-value action in the business.
3. **The portal first-entry screen — designed explicitly for emptiness.** The
   client will log in the day they pay, and there are no visits. The screen must
   never show a generic empty state. It shows, in this order:
   - a calm confirmation of what they have: *"Your Optimal plan is active. 4 visits over the next 12 months."*
   - **a dated schedule** — the first visit's planned window, then the remaining
     windows as month ranges. A date on a screen is what converts "I paid and
     nothing happened" into "I am waiting for something specific."
   - what will arrive after each visit, shown as a **preview of a real report
     layout** with placeholder content clearly labelled *"This is what your first
     report will look like."* Showing the shape of the future deliverable is the
     antidote to the void.
   - two named humans with direct numbers, on the screen, not behind a menu.
   - the guarantees, one line each, linked.
   - **no upsell of any kind.**
4. **The day before the first visit — an opt-in reminder**, which can be
   addressed to a *different* person: a relative in Yerevan who will meet the
   crew. Opt-in, per the portal rules; the choice of recipient is part of the
   subscription setup, not buried in profile settings.
5. **If the visit slips — we tell them first.** A postponed visit that we
   announce is a trust event in our favour; a postponed visit the client
   discovers is a refund request. The "visit postponed by weather" and "crew
   could not access the plot" screens are conversion assets, not edge cases: each
   must state the reason, the new date, and that the visit count is unchanged.

**Analytics instruction:** measure time-from-payment-to-first-report and
first-login-to-first-report separately. If that gap exceeds ~14 days, add a
sixth signal (a scheduling confirmation with the crew's planned date). Do not
solve it with a marketing email.

# B. UI implications

Only where conversion dictates the visual decision. Everything else is the
design lead's.

**B1. Above the fold at 375px — the single most contested space on the site.**
In roughly 640 CSS px of visible height, in this order and nothing else:
1. Header: MemoryCare mark + tap-to-call icon (the phone is a CTA for this audience).
2. H1 that disambiguates from dementia care in its own words.
3. One line of subhead naming the outcome and, implicitly, both audiences.
4. **The proof card** — a report fragment showing a GPS coordinate, a map pin, a
   date and a video-play affordance. It must be *visible*, not below the fold.
   This is the entire hero argument.
5. Primary CTA button, full-width, Deep Olive fill with Ivory label (6.01).

What is **not** allowed above the fold: the logo used as decorative hero art
(the current site's mistake), a carousel, a cookie banner covering the CTA, or a
before/after image.

**B2. Button hierarchy is a contrast decision, and the palette forces it.**
Primary = Deep Olive `#575E3B` fill, Ivory `#F3F0E9` label. Secondary = Deep
Olive 1.5px outline, Deep Olive label on Nude. Tertiary = Deep Olive text link,
underlined. **An Olive `#7C8654` button with a light label fails at 3.42 and is
forbidden** — this is a conversion issue before it is an accessibility issue: our
buyer is 40–60 and reading in bad light. On the Anthracite band, primary inverts
to Nude fill with an Anthracite label (9.61).

**B3. Surface assignment, fixed once (Nude and Ivory differ by 1.1 and will
otherwise be used interchangeably):** **Nude `#EFE5D5` is the page ground.
Ivory `#F3F0E9` is cards, form fields, and text on Anthracite.** A card is
therefore *lighter* than the page — which is what makes a pricing card and a
form field read as an interactive object without a border. Written down here so
it can be enforced in review.

**B4. Prominence order on the pricing page.** Optimal is emphasised by
*elevation and space*, not by size: same card width, more internal padding, an
Olive top band with Anthracite text carrying "Most chosen". Do not scale it up —
an enlarged card reads as a hard sell and this brand cannot carry that.

**B5. The Inspection card must be visually a different species**, not a smaller
sibling: different band, Ivory card on Nude, an Olive rule above it, small-caps
`ONE-OFF · NOT A SUBSCRIPTION`, and its price set at the same type size as the
subscription prices.

**B6. Sticky mobile CTA bar.** Appears after the hero leaves the viewport, 56px,
Ivory-on-Nude bar with a Deep Olive full-width button and a call icon at the
left. Suppressed on the sample-report page, in the portal, and on all legal
pages. Never overlaps the form's submit button.

# C. Content implications

The specific claims and proof elements each block must carry. Every claim below
is defensible with something that exists or will exist by first visit.

**C1. Hero**
- Claim: professional, scheduled care of a family grave in Yerevan, with proof of
  every visit.
- Must disambiguate from dementia care in the H1 itself, and in `<title>` and
  meta description.
- Proof element: report fragment with GPS coordinate, map pin, date, video affordance.
- Forbidden: superlatives, "the only", "the first", any number of clients, any
  "since 20XX".

**C2. How it works — three steps**
- Step 1: *We find and record your plot.* Proof: the GPS point is recorded on the
  first visit and checked on every visit after.
- Step 2: *We do the full work.* Proof: named method and equipment — steam,
  pressure washer, industrial vacuum, professional stone chemistry chosen for the
  material.
- Step 3: *You get the report.* Proof: photographs, video, coordinates, date,
  crew lead; arrives in the portal and by link.
- One line of policy: *every visit is a full visit.* No "light", no "preventive",
  no "monthly".

**C3. Sample report — the heaviest content block on the site**
- Must be a **real screen, explorable**, not a screenshot in a frame. Half our
  visitors' entire judgement is formed here.
- Must carry, in the report's own order: calm confirmation first (date, plot,
  status), photographs below; before/after available but never the opening image.
- Must state honestly what verification means: coordinates recorded and matched,
  timestamped media, video walk-around. Do not claim tamper-proof or notarised.
- Must show the share affordance and say plainly that a relative can open the
  report by link with no login — and that the link preview shows only the mark,
  "Visit report" and the date, never a photograph of a grave.
- Zero prices, zero upsell in this block.

**C4. Family Circle**
- Claim: relatives get their own accounts by invitation, see every report, and
  can order one-off services.
- Must carry the **permission distinction in plain words** — it is the proof that
  this is a real system and not a shared password: who views reports, who can
  order a one-off, who can change or cancel the subscription, who can invite
  others. State that only the subscription owner can change or cancel.
- Positioning line, accurate and safe: *"Photographs, video, GPS, a portal and
  accounts for the whole family — the full chain in one place."* Never "the only
  service that…".

**C5. Trust / About**
- Two named founders with photographs, roles, and their real published mobile
  numbers; info@memorycare.am; MemoryCare LLC; legal address as a clearly-marked
  placeholder flagged as an open item.
- Describe the crew, training and equipment. Do not invent certifications,
  insurance policies or partnerships that do not exist.
- Competitive line, if one is needed at all: describe what we do. Never a
  comparison table against hush.am on a site with no track record — it invites
  the reader to go and look at their 72 reviews.

**C6. Pricing**
- The honest-price sentence verbatim near the top.
- Full service descriptions per product (bank requirement and buyer requirement).
- Prices in AMD with the currency in words and the ֏ symbol; approximate FX
  clearly marked.
- Credit rules stated exactly and once, in the words given in A2.
- Plot coverage (16 m², 2 monuments) on the cards; surcharges published in the
  calculator.
- Payment reality stated plainly: bank transfer to the company account now, card
  payment when the bank enables it, **no date promised**.

**C7. Guarantees** — as drafted verbatim in A5, including the honest-limits
paragraph and links to the four legal pages.

**C8. Post-payment and portal copy**
- First-entry screen: plan name, visit count, dated schedule, report preview
  labelled as a preview, two named humans, guarantees. No upsell.
- Bad-news screens in the client's language, never in system language: *"Your
  visit on 14 September was postponed — heavy rain at Tokhmakh. The new date is
  17 September. Your plan still includes all 4 visits."* Error states on report
  screens carry no emoji, no "Oops", no "Something went wrong" — they say what
  failed, that the report is safe, and how to reach a person.
- Cancellation flow copy states the pro-rata amount as a number before the
  confirm step, and completes without a phone call.

**C9. Global forbidden list**
- Any testimonial, review count, client count, family count, or years in business.
- "Bestseller", "monthly", "light visit", "preventive visit".
- Any mention of a QR code on a headstone or a digital memory page, including
  "coming soon".
- "The only", "the first", "nobody else in Yerevan".
- Diaspora-only framing: "across the miles", "cheaper than flying home".
- Any price or upsell on a report screen or a guest report view.
- Any delivery date we have not agreed.
